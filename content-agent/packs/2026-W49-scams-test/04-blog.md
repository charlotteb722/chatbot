# AI is making scam emails more convincing: how UK small businesses can protect themselves

**Suggested meta description (under 155 characters):** AI is making scam emails harder to spot. Simple, free ways UK sole traders and small businesses can pause, check and report suspicious messages.

---

For years, many scam emails gave themselves away. Odd spelling, strange greetings, a logo that didn't look quite right. That's changing, and AI is a big part of the reason.

The UK's data protection regulator, the ICO, says cyber criminals are increasingly using AI to carry out attacks that are faster, more advanced and harder to detect [F1]. For a small business, where one person might handle email, payments and customer records, that matters.

## What's different now

The ICO describes several AI-powered tricks. Three are especially relevant to small businesses:

- **Convincing, personalised emails.** Attackers use AI to write messages that impersonate colleagues, clients or trusted suppliers [F2].
- **Fake voices and video.** AI-generated audio and video can be used to impersonate colleagues or IT staff, to trick people into resetting passwords or granting access [F3].
- **Faster password attacks.** AI speeds up attempts to break in, making weak or reused passwords more vulnerable [F4].

## Pause, check, report

You don't need a technical team to protect yourself. A few habits go a long way.

### Pause

GOV.UK's advice is simple: don't give out private information such as bank details or passwords, download attachments or click links in emails if you're not sure they're genuine [F10]. If a message feels urgent, that's a reason to slow down, not speed up.

### Check

This is my suggestion rather than official wording, but it's worth making a rule: if anyone asks you to change bank details, make an urgent payment or share login details, check by another route. Ring the person on a number you already have, not one given in the message. For phone or video requests that seem unusual, call back on a known number before acting.

### Report

Forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk, and suspicious text messages to 7726, which is free [F11]. If you think you've lost money or been hacked, contact Report Fraud if you're in England or Wales, or report it to Police Scotland if you're in Scotland [F12].

## Lock the door: four quick wins

The ICO's guidance for organisations includes some steps that suit even a one-person business:

1. **Turn on multi-factor authentication for your email**, and use strong passwords [F5]. This means a code or app check as well as your password.
2. **Keep software up to date**, so security fixes are applied promptly [F9].
3. **Hold less data.** Only collect and keep the personal data you genuinely need. The less you hold, the less there is to steal [F8].
4. **Have a simple plan.** Know who to contact if something goes wrong, and keep key contact details somewhere offline in case your systems are affected [F7].

## Free help for small businesses

GOV.UK's cyber security guidance for businesses points to several free resources [F13, F14, F15]:

- **Top Tips for Staff:** free online training that takes less than 30 minutes [F13]. The ICO recommends regular training so people can recognise AI-generated phishing, voice cloning and deepfakes [F6].
- **Cyber Action Toolkit:** clear, bite-sized actions for sole traders and small businesses [F14].
- **A free 30-minute session with a Cyber Advisor:** GOV.UK says small and medium sized businesses can currently get one [F15].
- **Cyber Essentials:** a government-backed scheme that protects against the most common online threats [F16].

Here's an illustration. It's a made-up example, not a real business. A two-person design studio agrees that any request to change bank details is checked by phone, switches on multi-factor authentication for both email accounts, and does the free staff training together over a coffee.
