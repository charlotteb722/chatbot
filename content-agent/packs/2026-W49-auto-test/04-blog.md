Picture this. An email arrives that runs to several pages, quotes laws from other countries and asks for "every piece of data you hold about me". It reads as if a chatbot wrote it, and it probably did. Do you still have to deal with it?

Yes. Here's what the UK's data protection regulator, the ICO, says, and a simple way to handle it.

## What's changed

A subject access request (SAR) is when someone asks for a copy of the personal information an organisation holds about them. The ICO says people are increasingly using AI to help them make these requests, and on 11 November 2026 it published guidance on how to handle them [F1].

The key point: a request isn't invalid just because a person used AI to help draft it. You must treat it in the same way as any other SAR [F2]. AI-written requests can be lengthy, repetitive or unclear, and may include irrelevant legal references or laws from other jurisdictions [F3]. None of that makes them invalid.

## Five calm steps

### 1. Recognise it

There are no formal requirements for a valid request. Someone can make one verbally or in writing, including on social media, and to any part of your business [F12]. Any member of your team could receive one [F12], so the ICO says all staff should have general training to help them recognise a SAR [F9].

### 2. Note the date

You must respond as soon as possible and no later than one month. The month starts when you receive the request, plus any ID or proof of authority you've asked for [F4].

Don't assume a long request gives you extra time. The ICO says a SAR isn't complex simply because it's lengthy, unclear or full of legal references. If it's genuinely complex, you can extend by up to a further two months, but you must contact the person and explain why [F5].

### 3. Check it's genuine

This is where AI cuts both ways. The ICO warns that fraudsters may use AI to create false ID or letters of authority to get hold of someone's personal information [F8]. If a request comes from a third party and the letter of authority looks doubtful, contact the person the request is about directly [F8].

### 4. Clarify only if you need to

If a request is genuinely unclear, you can ask for more information. Only ask if it's reasonably required, and you can't make the person narrow their request [F6]. The clock pauses while you wait for their reply and restarts when you receive it [F6].

You can also explain, politely, if a request seems to misunderstand how data protection law works, possibly because of the way it was generated [F10].

### 5. Search sensibly and send securely

You must make a reasonable and proportionate search, but you don't have to carry out searches that are unreasonable or disproportionate [F7]. In most cases you can't charge a fee [F14].

When you send the information, keep it secure. The ICO suggests, for example, a password-protected file with the password sent in a separate email [F15]. If the request came in on social media, don't reply with the information there. Ask for other delivery details [F13].

## Make future requests easier

The ICO suggests a few practical steps that suit even very small businesses: give people clear guidance on how to make a request and a point of contact, publish optional example templates, and explain your response times [F11].

A sole trader running a small online shop adds a short "Your data" section to their privacy page, with an email address for requests and a line saying they'll reply within one month. Requests become easier to spot and easier to answer.

## Your next step

This week, tell everyone who handles your emails, phone or social media that a data request can arrive through any of them, and agree who deals with it. It's a small conversation that avoids a missed deadline.
